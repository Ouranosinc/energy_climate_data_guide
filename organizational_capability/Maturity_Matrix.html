Organizational Maturity Self-Assessment

Summary

The Climate Data Maturity Matrix is an internal self-assessment tool that helps energy-sector organizations examine the capabilities and practices they rely on to use climate information. It covers organizational capacity and governance, climate-risk understanding, and integration into planning, together with the acquisition, evaluation, and fitness-for-purpose use of climate information and the management of its limitations and uncertainty.

The matrix provides a common basis for discussing current practices, identifying gaps, and planning improvements. Results should be interpreted within a clearly defined assessment scope and in relation to its responsibilities, risks, and decisions. They are not intended to benchmark organizations, determine regulatory compliance, or produce an external organizational rating. Accordingly, Level 5 is not necessarily the appropriate target for every criterion or assessment scope.

Download the Maturity Matrix (Excel)

What the matrix is for

The matrix supports internal reflection and dialogue about the capabilities needed to use climate information responsibly and effectively. It can help an organization:

  • Identify strengths, gaps, and dependencies across organizational and technical practices;
  • Clarify roles, responsibilities, and evidence;
  • Set maturity targets proportionate to specific responsibilities and risks;
  • Prioritize practical improvements; and
  • Revisit progress periodically.

The matrix does not measure corporate climate ambition, regulatory compliance, or absolute modelling sophistication. A high score in one criterion does not compensate automatically for a limitation elsewhere. Similarly, mature practice does not necessarily require the most complex model, automated system, or technically advanced method. Methods and processes should be proportionate to their intended application and consequences.

Choose the assessment scope

Define the organizational scope before completing the matrix. The scope may be the whole organization when practices are genuinely shared, or a specific business function, unit, or major operational area.

Vertically integrated organizations should normally assess materially different functions separately—for example, generation, transmission, distribution, system operations, or corporate planning—and compare the resulting profiles rather than forcing them into one score. Record which teams, activities, decisions, and climate-information types are included and excluded.

A centralized climate group, shared service, consultant, or data provider may contribute to the assessed unit’s capability. Rate how reliably the unit can access, use, oversee, and retain the benefit of that support. The existence of a central group or contractual relationship does not automatically give every supported unit the same maturity.

In this matrix, climate information includes observations, reanalyses, weather and climate forecasts, climate-model projections, derived indicators, and related analytical products. Individual criteria apply only to the information types included in the assessment scope. Product-specific terms such as forecast, projection, hindcast, scenario, and observation are used where the distinction matters.

Who should participate

Complete the matrix as a cross-functional exercise rather than as an isolated questionnaire. Depending on the assessment scope, participants may include representatives from:

  • Planning and operations;
  • Engineering and asset management;
  • Risk, resilience, or adaptation;
  • Climate services and data analysis;
  • Data governance and information technology; and
  • External contractors or consultants responsible for relevant technical work.

Some criteria in Sections D–G require technical evidence. Involve personnel who perform or oversee that work. Where a contractor performs the analysis, assess the organization’s ability to specify requirements, review evidence, retain documentation, and exercise appropriate oversight. Use Not assessed rather than guessing when the necessary evidence or expertise is unavailable.

What is in the Excel workbook?

With the assessment scope and participants identified, the workbook provides a structured way to document evidence, assess current practice, and identify improvements.

The workbook contains four complementary worksheets:

Worksheet Purpose
Instructions & Scope Explains how to complete the assessment and records its organizational scope, included climate-information types, participants, and evidence sources.
Assessment Contains the 37 maturity criteria, their Level 1–5 descriptions, implementation status, current and desired maturity levels, and supporting evidence.
Summary Provides descriptive section-level results, including the median, score range, number of scored criteria, and implementation-status counts. It does not calculate an overall organizational score.
Glossary Defines key terms used in the workbook to support consistent interpretation across participants and assessment scopes.

Begin with Instructions & Scope, then complete the Assessment worksheet. Use Summary to review the resulting profile and consult Glossary whenever clarification is needed.

Before assigning implementation status or maturity levels, review the seven sections to understand the organizational and technical capabilities covered by the assessment.

Understanding the seven sections

A — Organizational Capacity, Governance and Resources: Assesses whether the defined scope has reliable governance, expertise, external relationships, funding, staff capacity, training, and accountability mechanisms.

B — Climate Risk Understanding and Organizational Readiness: Assesses whether people have the climate-risk understanding appropriate to their roles and whether that understanding supports risk management and adaptation.

C — Planning and Deployment Strategy: Assesses how climate information is incorporated into organizational planning horizons, assumptions, coordination, tools, procedures, and learning. Planning and operations refer to activities, not necessarily teams with those names.

D — Data Acquisition, Provenance and Initial Screening: Assesses whether the origin, conditions of use, processing history, version, metadata, and basic integrity of acquired climate information are understood and retained.

E — Quality Control and Validation: Assesses whether climate information and relevant derived outputs undergo proportionate technical evaluation before use. Appropriate methods depend on product type, application, available reference information, and decision consequences.

F — Fitness for Purpose and Constraints: Assesses whether climate information is suitable for a particular use and whether material constraints and trade-offs are understood. Technically credible information may still be unsuitable for a specific decision.

G — Understanding and Managing Data Limitations and Uncertainty: Assesses whether gaps, errors, assumptions, uncertainty, input sensitivity, and misuse risks are understood and reflected in applications and communication.

Although limitations and uncertainty are addressed explicitly in Section G, they should be considered throughout acquisition, evaluation, fitness-for-purpose assessment, application, and communication.

How the Maturity Matrix Sections Work Together

Distinguishing data screening, validation, and fitness for purpose

Sections D, E, and F address related but distinct questions about climate information: what the information is and whether it passes initial screening, whether its quality and performance have been evaluated, and whether it is suitable for the intended decision. The table below clarifies these differences.

Section Central question Example
D — Acquisition and initial screening Do we know what the information is, where it came from, and whether it has obvious structural or integrity problems? Verify source, version, licensing, units, coverage, missing values, and file integrity.
E — Quality control and validation Has its quality or performance been evaluated appropriately? Compare relevant characteristics with appropriate reference information and evaluate consequential biases or predictive performance.
F — Fitness for purpose Is it suitable for this particular decision? Determine whether spatial scale, variables, indicators, future conditions, and constraints match the use case.

How to complete the matrix

1. Select implementation status

Status and maturity are recorded separately.

Status Meaning
Implemented The capability is currently in use within the assessment scope.
In development Work is actively underway but is not yet established in normal practice.
Not started The capability is relevant, but no material implementation work has begun.
Not assessed The assessment team lacks sufficient evidence or expertise to determine the current state.
Not applicable The criterion is not relevant to the defined scope or climate-information types. Provide a brief justification.

For In development, score only the capability currently demonstrated in practice and describe planned improvements in Evidence and justification. If no level is currently demonstrated, the current score may remain blank. A blank current score is also permitted for Not started, Not assessed, or Not applicable when no maturity level can be supported. Explain blank scores and Not applicable selections.

2. Apply guided best fit

The five maturity levels are:

Level General interpretation
Level 1 — Initial Practice is absent, informal, inconsistent, or dependent on individual effort.
Level 2 — Developing Relevant activity occurs, but coverage, consistency, documentation, or ownership remains limited.
Level 3 — Defined A repeatable and documented approach is established for relevant applications within the assessment scope.
Level 4 — Integrated The approach is consistently embedded across relevant workflows, with clear ownership, coordination, and review.
Level 5 — Adaptive The approach is sustained, evaluated, and improved as needs, evidence, and conditions change.

Select the highest level whose description is substantially and consistently demonstrated. Do not select a higher level when an essential underlying capability is absent. If practice spans several levels, choose the lower defensible level and document the more advanced elements. Base the score on normal practice and evidence, not an isolated example, planned activity, or aspiration.

Useful evidence may include policies, procedures, project records, data inventories, validation reports, meeting records, training materials, risk registers, decision documents, or demonstrations from staff and contractors.

3. Set desired maturity

Desired maturity need not be Level 5. Set it criterion by criterion according to:

  • The assessed unit’s responsibilities;
  • The consequences and materiality of the decisions involved;
  • The frequency and importance of climate-information use;
  • Regulatory, reporting, or assurance requirements;
  • Access to centralized or external expertise; and
  • The risks associated with misuse, failure, or insufficient capability.

A lower desired maturity may be appropriate where responsibilities are limited and reliable escalation arrangements exist. Record the rationale for the desired level and the priority of any gap.

How to interpret the results

The criterion-level profile is the primary result. Do not calculate a single overall organizational maturity score.

The workbook provides an optional descriptive summary for each section, including:

  • Median current maturity;
  • Minimum and maximum criterion scores;
  • Number of scored criteria; and
  • Counts for each implementation status.

A section median is a discussion aid, not evidence that strength in one criterion compensates for limitations in another. Interpret it alongside the full criterion profile, score range, status counts, and supporting evidence. Do not compare summaries when assessment scopes, applicable criteria, or climate-information types differ materially.

Turn the results into an improvement plan

Use the assessment to identify a focused and achievable set of improvements. Prioritize gaps based on:

  • Consequences for important operational, planning, or investment decisions;
  • Material climate risks and organizational exposure;
  • Dependencies among capabilities;
  • Organizational responsibility and desired maturity;
  • Available resources and feasibility; and
  • Relevant planning and investment timelines.

Some improvements may involve clearer roles, documentation, or procedures; others may require sustained investment in staffing, expertise, data, tools, or validation. Assign accountability, identify evidence of completion, and revisit the assessment periodically. Progress should mean more reliable, transparent, and decision-appropriate practice, and not simply movement toward Level 5 in every criterion.

Use the matrix to identify areas for organizational improvement, then consult Organizational Gaps and Barriers for common challenges affecting climate-information use. Where external expertise is needed, Procuring Physical Climate-Risk Assessments provides questions to support informed procurement and oversight.
