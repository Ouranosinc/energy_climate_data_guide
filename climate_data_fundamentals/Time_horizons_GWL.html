Projection Periods and Global Warming Levels

Future climate information is commonly organized in two complementary ways: by a specified future projection period or by the global warming level reached. A projection period describes climate conditions during a defined calendar window, whereas a global warming level describes regional or local conditions associated with a specified increase in global mean temperature. The distinction matters because different climate models and emissions scenarios may reach the same warming level at different times. Neither framing is universally preferable; the appropriate choice depends on the purpose and timing of the analysis.

For an explanation of how climate projections differ from weather, seasonal and decadal forecasts, see Forecasts, Projections and Predictability. Detailed discussions of emissions scenarios, climate-model ensembles and uncertainty are provided on the neighboring pages.

Projection Periods

Projection periods are multi-year or multi-decadal calendar windows used to summarize projected climate conditions. Climate analyses commonly use periods of 20 or 30 years so that statistics describe climate rather than individual years. Thirty-year periods are consistent with the World Meteorological Organization’s guidance on climatological standard normals. ClimateData.ca also provides an interactive explanation of the importance of using 30 years of data.

Common labels include the near term or 2030s, mid-century or 2050s, and end of century or 2080s. These labels are shorthand and must be accompanied by the exact years represented because conventions vary. For example, an assessment might use:

  • Historical baseline: a 30-year period representing historical or recent climate conditions, such as 1991–2020.
  • Near-term projection period (2030s): 2021–2050.
  • Mid-term projection period (2050s): 2041–2070.
  • Long-term projection period (2080s): 2071–2100.

The start and end years may differ among assessments, and projection periods may overlap. The historical baseline must also be stated explicitly so that projected changes can be interpreted correctly.

Projection periods are particularly useful when an analysis is tied to a calendar date, planning cycle or asset lifetime. A near-term operational plan and a long-lived generation, transmission or water-management asset may require different periods. The projection period used for climate analysis should therefore align, as far as practical, with the planning horizon relevant to the decision.

Results for a given projection period depend on the emissions scenario and on how individual climate models respond to the associated forcing. Differences among emissions scenarios generally become more important later in the century, while model response and natural variability also contribute to the range of projected conditions. Analyses extending beyond 2100 require particular care because fewer simulations are available and uncertainty is high.

Global Warming Levels

A global warming level (GWL) represents a specified increase in global mean surface temperature relative to pre-industrial conditions, commonly the 1850–1900 reference period. Examples include 1.5°C, 2°C, 3°C and 4°C of global warming. Rather than asking about climate during one calendar period, a GWL analysis asks what regional or local climate conditions are associated with a world that has reached the selected amount of global warming.

For each climate-model simulation, a 20- or 30-year window is generally selected around the time the prescribed warming level is reached. Models respond to forcing at different rates, and emissions scenarios follow different trajectories. Consequently, the calendar years associated with a GWL usually differ among models and scenarios. A higher-forcing scenario may reach a selected level earlier than a lower-forcing scenario, while some scenarios or simulations may not reach higher warming levels within the period available for analysis.

Aligning simulations by warming level allows regional changes to be compared at a common amount of global warming. It can also allow simulations from multiple emissions scenarios to contribute to an ensemble when they reach the selected level. This may increase the available ensemble size and can reduce some of the spread caused by comparing models at different amounts of warming during a fixed period. The approach can include models with a strong temperature response, sometimes described as “hot models” (Hausfather et al. 2022), provided they are interpreted at the selected warming level.

These advantages do not eliminate uncertainty. Simulations aligned at the same GWL share a similar global mean temperature change, but they do not necessarily produce identical regional climates. Differences in model structure, natural variability and regional responses remain. A GWL framing also does not identify one calendar date at which the associated conditions will occur: the timing depends on the model and scenario. Results should therefore retain information about the simulations included and the periods selected for them.

GWLs provide a direct connection to mitigation goals expressed in terms of limiting global temperature increase, including 1.5°C and 2°C. Comparing the Two Approaches

Framing Defined by Particularly useful when Important consideration
Projection period A specified calendar window, such as 2041–2070 The decision is tied to a date, planning cycle or asset lifetime Results depend on the emissions scenario, model response and exact years selected
Global warming level A specified increase in global mean temperature relative to a reference period Comparing regional changes or impacts consistently at a common amount of global warming The associated calendar period differs among models and scenarios, and some simulations may not reach the selected level

The approaches answer different questions. A projection period asks, What climate conditions may occur during this future time window? A global warming level asks, What regional or local conditions are associated with this amount of global warming? A GWL can be translated into model- and scenario-specific calendar windows, but it should not be treated as a single future date.

Choosing an Approach for an Application

For an electricity-sector application, consider how the climate information will be used:

  • Use a projection period when the decision is tied to a specific date, regulatory cycle, planning horizon or asset lifetime.
  • Consider a global warming level when the objective is to compare impacts at a common amount of warming, connect the analysis with temperature-based mitigation goals, or compare with assessments already expressed using GWLs.
  • Preserve scenario-dependent timing when the timing of impacts, investments or adaptation actions is important.
  • State the exact projection years or GWL definition, reference period, averaging window, scenarios and models used so that results can be interpreted and compared correctly.

Some applications may benefit from presenting both framings. For example, projection periods can support date-specific planning while GWLs help explain how the same regional changes relate to global temperature outcomes. The choice should be documented rather than assumed, and no single approach is appropriate for every electricity-sector assessment.
