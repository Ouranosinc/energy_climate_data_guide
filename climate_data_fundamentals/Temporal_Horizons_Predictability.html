Forecasts, Projections and Predictability

Future climate information is produced across multiple timescales. Weather forecasts, subseasonal and seasonal forecasts, decadal predictions and climate projections answer different questions, draw predictability from different parts of the climate system, and require different interpretation. In this guide, a forecast or prediction is initialized from an estimate of the current state to predict how conditions may evolve, whereas a projection describes possible future climate under specified assumptions about external forcing. The principal timescales are:

  • Weather forecasts (1-14 days)
  • Sub-seasonal forecasts (2-6 weeks)
  • Seasonal forecasts (1-12 months)
  • Decadal forecasts (1-10 years)
  • Climate projections (+10 years; typically up to end of the century)

There are many differences between these simulations, ranging from how they are produced to the type of information that they provide to how they should be evaluated. The first is the source of predictability: the longer the simulation, the less its forecast skill depends on the initial state of the system. Weather prediction depends strongly on the state of the atmosphere at the beginning of the simulation, which is referred to as an initial-value problem. Predictability at subseasonal timescales also draws on land conditions such as soil moisture and snow cover and on stratospheric conditions. Seasonal predictability draws on atmospheric and oceanic variations such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, while decadal predictions draw on slowly changing ocean conditions.

At longer timescales, changes in atmospheric composition and their effect on the Earth’s energy balance become more important; this is referred to as a forced boundary-value problem. Climate projections are not initialized to predict the exact sequence of future weather. Their long-term interpretation is instead conditional on assumptions about external forcing, which are described on the Emissions Scenarios page. Initial conditions nevertheless select different realizations of natural climate variability. Running the same climate-model experiment from slightly different initial conditions produces members with different weather sequences, as explained under Climate-Model Ensembles. Thus, initial conditions do not provide long-term predictability of a particular weather sequence, but they remain useful for sampling internal variability.

Another important distinction is the type of information provided: only weather forecasts provide deterministic information (e.g. max/min temperature for each day of the forecast). For the other types of simulations, the information is typically aggregated over a specific time period and the statistics of that time period, and how it compares to a period of reference, are provided (e.g. +3 \(^{\circ}\)C on average compared to the pre-industrial era; 70% chance that winter will be warmer than normal). The calendar windows and warming-level framings used for climate projections are discussed in Projection Periods and Global Warming Levels.
